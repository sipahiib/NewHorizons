## Roman

NASA has just launched a new eye into space, and it could rewrite what we know about the universe. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off on August thirtieth aboard a Falcon Heavy rocket, beginning its journey towards the Sun–Earth L2 region, roughly one and a half million kilometres from our planet. Soon after launch, Roman began deploying and checking the systems it will need for years of precision observing.

Roman's defining advantage is not simply sharper vision. Its Wide Field Instrument can survey an area of sky at least one hundred times larger than Hubble can capture in a comparable exposure, while retaining Hubble-class detail. That combination turns painstaking snapshots into enormous cosmic maps. Astronomers will use those maps to study how galaxies grew, how matter is distributed, and why the expansion of the universe is accelerating.

The telescope will also hunt worlds around other stars. By watching dense star fields, Roman can detect the brief brightening caused when an unseen planet bends the light of a more distant star. This technique, called gravitational microlensing, is sensitive to planets that are difficult for many other surveys to find, including cold worlds far from their stars and possibly free-floating planets wandering through space.

Roman also carries a coronagraph technology demonstration designed to suppress the glare of a star and reveal faint material nearby. It is not the mission's main survey instrument, but what engineers learn could influence future telescopes built to image Earth-like planets directly.

The launch is only the beginning. Roman must complete its journey, commissioning, alignment, and instrument checks before routine science starts. If those steps go as planned, the mission will connect vast statistical surveys with detailed observations from telescopes such as Hubble and Webb. Instead of studying the universe through narrow windows, astronomers are about to open a panoramic one.

## Alpha Gal

A tick bite can trigger an allergy that appears hours after eating red meat. Now, researchers at the United States National Institutes of Health have identified rare monoclonal antibodies that blocked allergic IgE from binding to alpha-gal in laboratory tests. Alpha-gal is a sugar found in most mammals, and sensitisation can make foods, medicines, or animal-derived products dangerous for some people. The finding is promising because current care mainly depends on avoiding triggers and preparing for reactions. It is not yet a treatment, and the antibodies still require broader laboratory study and clinical testing. But they reveal a possible way to interrupt the reaction itself, giving scientists a clearer target for future therapies.

## Astra

OpenAI says Astra has crossed a critical cybersecurity threshold under the company's Preparedness Framework. With appropriate tools and access, the model can reportedly discover unknown software flaws and build working exploit chains. This could help defenders accelerate code review, vulnerability research, and incident response, and demand closer coordination between security teams and developers. The same capability creates serious misuse risks, so early access will be limited, monitored, and tied to strong controls. The shift is from benchmark performance towards operational capability. Progress will now be judged not only by what a model can find, but by who can use it and under what safeguards.

## Cluster

Europe's Cluster mission has delivered a rare view of a satellite's final moments. ESA guided the Samba spacecraft into a targeted atmospheric reentry over the South Pacific, while researchers aboard the ROSIE aircraft recorded the fiery breakup. Controllers predicted the final timing with remarkable precision, and the observations now provide real evidence of how spacecraft fragment and burn. That matters because engineers need more than computer models when designing satellites to disappear safely at the end of a mission. Data from Samba, and the planned Tango reentry that follows it, can improve design-for-demise techniques and estimates of atmospheric effects. A controlled farewell from an older mission could therefore make future spaceflight cleaner, safer, and more responsible.

## Pallas

China's commercial space sector has reached another milestone. Galactic Energy says its liquid-fuel PALLAS-1 rocket completed its maiden flight and reached the intended orbit. The first stage uses seven kerosene and liquid-oxygen engines, giving the company a larger vehicle than its earlier solid-fuel rockets. PALLAS-1 is intended to support vertical landing and reuse, although this first mission did not attempt a recovery. Reaching orbit on a debut validates major parts of propulsion, guidance, and staging. The next challenge is repeatability: turning one success into reliable operations, then proving that a returning booster can land and fly again.
