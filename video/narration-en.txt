## M1 — Mars Water Systems
Perseverance found a hidden water history written inside volcanic rock.

At the edge of Mars’s Jezero Crater, the rover examined the Margin Unit. Scientists expected layered lake-shore sediment. Instead, they found igneous rock altered repeatedly by water.

And four other breakthroughs happened this week: a cybersecurity AI project, factory automation plans, a dissolving medical battery, and a surprising Arctic trend.

The story begins with olivine-rich rock formed from magma deep underground. At higher elevations, its large crystals show almost no water alteration. Lower down, the olivine is fractured and transformed.

SuperCam examined many bedrock targets. Its laser creates plasma, revealing rock chemistry from several metres away.

That chemistry records at least three water episodes. First, carbon-dioxide-rich groundwater reacted with olivine and left carbonate inside fractures. A later interaction may have involved the lake that once filled Jezero Crater, leaving more silica below the ancient water line.

Finally, heated groundwater circulated through volcanic rock and produced mineral veins containing calcium sulfate and fluorite.

Carbonate and silica matter because, on Earth, they can preserve traces of ancient environments. Reactions between water and olivine can also release hydrogen, an energy source for some microbes.

But this is not evidence that life existed on Mars. The minerals document water interacting with rock, and scientists cannot yet date the three episodes. What the rover has revealed is a more complicated sequence of groundwater, lake water and hydrothermal activity than orbital observations alone suggested.

One place on Mars did not have a single water story. It became a crossroads for several.

## M2 — Cybersecurity AI
South Korea is moving artificial intelligence toward a highly specialised target: cybersecurity.

The Ministry of Science and ICT announced the selected project for developing a cybersecurity-focused foundation model. The idea reflects a wider shift away from general chatbots toward systems trained and evaluated for a particular operational domain.

Security work demands more than fluent answers. A useful system must recognise malicious behaviour, work with sensitive technical data, distinguish genuine threats from false alarms, and remain resistant to manipulation by attackers.

For viewers, the practical point is that specialisation may make AI more useful—but also easier to over-trust. This announcement is a development selection, not a finished product or proof that attacks will be stopped. Capability, false-positive rates, data protection and performance against new threats still need independent testing.

## M3 — Factory AI
Korean manufacturers are starting AI plans with factory-floor problems.

At a government-backed hackathon, around one hundred participants from manufacturing, artificial-intelligence companies and related organisations developed possible projects for regional industrial sites.

Proposals included predictive maintenance, computer-vision inspection, capturing workers’ know-how as training data, and process simulations. Teams also examined which process to target, what data and investment would be needed, and whether a project could move into a supported demonstration.

That offers a practical lesson: successful factory AI begins with a measurable bottleneck, reliable data and a deployment plan—not with adding AI everywhere.

These were proposals and examples, not independently measured productivity gains. Follow-up demonstrations must still show whether the systems work safely and economically in real factories.

## M4 — Bioresorbable Battery
A battery inspired by edible rice paper could make temporary medical electronics safer.

Researchers built a cellulose-supported battery from magnesium, molybdenum trioxide, biodegradable electrolyte and beeswax. In pigs, it powered an identification tag that transmitted a signal outside the body. A second device electrically stimulated the stomach and increased levels of the hunger-related hormone ghrelin.

In simulated gastric fluid, the battery degraded over months. The goal is to avoid batteries that can leak corrosive or toxic materials.

One day, this approach might power ingestible sensors or temporary implants without requiring removal.

But the experiments were performed in pigs, not people. They do not establish clinical benefit, long-term human safety or regulatory approval. The battery is a promising preclinical power source—not a treatment ready for patients.

## M5 — Arctic Melt Season
The Arctic sea-ice melt season is about forty days longer than it was in nineteen seventy-nine. Yet its average length appears to have stopped increasing around twenty ten.

A NASA-led study analysed satellite observations through twenty twenty-three. Earlier extension came mostly from later autumn freeze-up, as darker open ocean absorbed more solar energy.

Since twenty ten, changing cloud patterns have reduced incoming sunlight in parts of the Arctic. Year-to-year weather now has greater influence over ice that is generally younger, thinner and more vulnerable.

This does not mean Arctic warming has stopped. Melt-season length is only one measure; remaining ice is much thinner, and the region continues to warm far faster than the global average.

Researchers warn the pause may be temporary. Continued thinning north of Greenland and the Canadian Arctic could trigger rapid change. The real lesson is that a flat trend can hide a fragile system.
