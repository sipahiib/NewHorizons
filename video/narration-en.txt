## M1 — Polar Ice Sheets
Most polar ice loss wasn’t surface melt—it was glaciers speeding into the ocean.

Satellite records show the ice sheets losing mass as frozen rivers carry ice towards the sea.

Four more important developments are coming, from surgical AI to asteroid chemistry. Let’s begin with the biggest.

A new international assessment reconstructed how the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets changed between 1979 and 2023. The Ice Sheet Mass Balance Inter-comparison Exercise combined forty-two estimates from twenty-seven satellite missions.

Together, the two ice sheets lost an estimated eleven thousand three hundred and nine gigatonnes, with an uncertainty of five hundred and sixty-five gigatonnes. Researchers calculated that this added thirty-one point four millimetres to global mean sea level, plus or minus one point six millimetres.

The striking result is where most loss came from. Eighty-four percent was attributed to an imbalance in ice dynamics, mainly glaciers flowing faster and discharging more ice into the ocean. Surface changes, including snowfall and melting, accounted for the remaining sixteen percent. So the headline cannot be described simply as surface melting.

Greenland and Antarctica did not lose ice at a constant rate. A slowdown from 2020 to 2023 reflects short-term variability, not a reversal of the long-term trend.

Why does this matter beyond the poles? Higher global mean sea level raises the baseline for coastal flooding and erosion. Local effects also depend on tides, storms and land movement, but a longer record helps refine projections used for coastal planning.

This assessment concerns the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, not sea ice or every glacier. By combining decades of observations and reporting uncertainty, it provides a clearer view of how Earth’s largest ice sheets are changing.

## M2 — Surgical Navigation AI
In medicine, MIT-led researchers developed a patient-specific artificial-intelligence system called x-v-r. It matches two-dimensional X-rays taken during a procedure with a patient’s three-dimensional CT or MRI scan prepared beforehand.

The team reports that the system can adapt to one patient in about five minutes, then register new X-ray images in seconds with sub-millimetre precision. It was evaluated using real data from five hospitals, including adult and paediatric cases.

That alignment could eventually give clinicians more reliable spatial guidance during minimally invasive procedures, connecting a limited X-ray view with richer information in the earlier scan.

But this remains a research system, not an autonomous surgeon. The study does not show improved patient outcomes or establish clinical approval. Moving anatomy and broader conditions still need testing. The promise is future guidance—not treatment available today.

## M3 — Artificial Leaf
In Singapore, Nanyang Technological University researchers built an artificial leaf that tackles two problems at once. Powered by sunlight, the photoelectrochemical device produces hydrogen from seawater while breaking down hydrazine, a toxic industrial contaminant.

In simulated and real seawater, it maintained stable photocurrent for more than seventy-two hours. NTU reports hydrazine falling from zero point five molar to zero point five parts per billion within thirty hours.

A future system might combine wastewater treatment with solar-fuel production.

However, this is a laboratory result, not a commercial hydrogen plant. The tests do not establish long-term durability, safe industrial scale-up or competitive costs. Hydrazine is hazardous and requires careful handling. The chemical pathway is promising; engineering a practical system is the larger challenge.

## M4 — TRPM2 and Pain
A temperature sensor may also be involved in chronic pain. Warwick researchers studied TRPM2, an ion channel that detects mild warmth, and found it can convert inflammatory and immune signals into pain-related electrical activity.

Removing TRPM2 from sensory nerves reduced pain behaviours in mouse models. In the arthritis experiment, blocking it with a joint injection reversed pain behaviour for two days without suppressing inflammation.

Instead of broadly reducing inflammation, a future therapy might target how sensory nerves translate inflammatory signals into pain.

But the result comes from mice and cells. It does not demonstrate a safe dose, effective human treatment or arthritis cure. Side effects and lasting benefit require extensive testing. TRPM2 is a possible target and a clue to pain signalling—not a medicine patients can receive.

## M5 — Ryugu Chemistry
Asteroid Ryugu samples revealed an under-recognised part of their chemistry. A Japanese collaboration used synchrotron X-rays and solubility experiments to identify poorly crystalline, magnesium-rich hydrous silicate resembling magnesium silicate hydrate, or M-S-H.

The material appears in Hayabusa2’s Ryugu samples and in carbonaceous meteorites. It may explain how these primitive materials were altered by water while preserving early Solar System chemical differences.

This is not evidence of oceans on Ryugu, but a record of water interacting with rock in its parent body.

The phrase “invisible matter” needs care. It does not mean dark matter, and this is not evidence of life. It describes a mineral component earlier methods had not adequately recognised. Returned samples let laboratories revisit ancient material with new techniques and uncover previously hidden chemistry.
