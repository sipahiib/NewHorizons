## FAST — 0:00–2:00

FAST just mapped 156,411 cosmic hydrogen sources. Built into a natural basin in southwest China, this enormous radio dish has surveyed nearly half the sky. Four more important developments are coming. Let's begin with the biggest.

On September eighth, the FAST survey team released its second public data set. It contains radio detections of neutral hydrogen beyond our own galaxy, gathered between 2020 and 2025.

Neutral hydrogen is not a message from space. It is the simplest form of the universe's most abundant element, and a basic reservoir from which galaxies make new stars. It emits a faint radio signature with a wavelength of about twenty-one centimetres. Unlike visible light, that signal can pass through clouds of interstellar dust.

FAST's huge collecting area is sensitive enough to catch those weak emissions across the nearby universe. The new catalogue gives each detection a position and measured signal, allowing astronomers to map where galaxy-forming gas is concentrated.

The real advance is scale. Researchers can compare gas-rich and gas-poor galaxies, study how matter is arranged across cosmic structures, and identify targets for follow-up observations. The catalogue is publicly available, so teams around the world can work with the measurements without repeating the entire survey.

Researchers can cross-match the radio catalogue with optical surveys, linking hydrogen to visible galaxies.

But this is a data release, not proof of a new cosmological theory. Its sources mostly belong to the relatively nearby, low-redshift universe, and each entry should not be described as a newly discovered galaxy. Even so, one telescope has turned years of faint radio signals into a shared map of the raw material of stars.

Now, from cosmic gas to the code of life.

## GPN-Star — 2:00–3:00

University of California, Berkeley researchers have built an AI model that learns about human DNA through evolution.

G P N Star compares our genome with those of other species. Sections preserved across evolution are more likely to perform important functions, so the model uses those patterns to flag genetic changes for closer attention.

Only a small fraction of human DNA directly codes for proteins. Much of the rest helps control gene activity, yet remains difficult to interpret.

The team says G P N Star can prioritise variants linked to inherited traits and disease, while using less computing than much larger genomic models.

The potential is faster, better-targeted research. But this is not a diagnostic test. A prediction does not prove that a variant causes disease, and promising results still need laboratory and clinical validation.

It narrows the search for researchers.

## Nitinol springs — 3:00–4:00

A custom metal spring has been used in surgery for a baby born with a rare skull condition.

Teams from U C L, Great Ormond Street Hospital and Durham University designed it for severe sagittal craniosynostosis, where part of the skull fuses too early and restricts space for the growing brain.

The spring uses nitinol, an alloy that can bend and return to a planned shape. With C T scans and computer modelling, the team tailored its force and placement. After surgeons made a narrow opening, the springs gradually widened it as new bone formed.

The first procedure took place in September 2025. The child was doing well when the result was announced this week.

It is promising engineering, but still one early patient result, not a comparative trial. In Britain, each use requires individual regulatory approval.

Longer follow-up is needed to judge how this approach works across children and complex cases.

## High-NA chipmaking — 4:00–5:00

Samsung and A S M L are expanding their collaboration on advanced chip manufacturing.

Their plan centres on High N A extreme ultraviolet lithography. It uses specialised optics and short-wavelength light to print smaller features onto silicon. Samsung intends to introduce High N A E U V into high-volume D RAM production by 2028.

The companies will also work on a proposed twelve-inch photomask platform. A larger format could reduce stitching constraints and improve factory productivity as chip designs become more complex.

This matters because memory and logic chips sit underneath AI systems, phones, vehicles and data centres. Manufacturing improvements can affect performance, supply and cost.

But this is a partnership and a roadmap, not a newly shipped chip. Neither the larger mask platform nor the 2028 production target is guaranteed, because implementation matters.

## Sentinel-6 — 5:00–6:00

Two satellites are flying over the same stretches of ocean just thirty seconds apart.

Sentinel Six Michael Freilich and Sentinel Six B are a joint mission of NASA, NOAA and European partners. They measure sea-surface height, waves and marine wind speed.

Warmer water expands, so ocean height can help researchers estimate where heat is stored below the surface. That matters for hurricanes because unusually warm, deep water can support rapid intensification.

The twin arrangement lets scientists compare measurements almost immediately and check the consistency of the long climate record.

For coastal communities, better observations could strengthen forecasting and emergency planning. But satellites do not predict hurricanes alone. Their measurements must be combined with atmospheric observations and models, and NASA has not claimed Sentinel Six B has already improved a particular forecast. Integration supports better warnings.
