## Neuroprosthesis — 0:00–2:00

A brain implant just decoded speech and body language at the same time. The result points toward assistive communication that could express more than words alone.

Four more important developments are coming, from a space telescope to robotics, ultrathin electronics and eye scans. Let's begin with the biggest.

At the University of California, San Francisco, researchers worked with three people who had paralysis. Each already had electrodes placed on the brain's surface.

Those electrodes record patterns from the motor cortex. They do not read every private thought. The system was trained for specific attempted phrases and familiar gestures, including a wave and a thumbs-up.

The participants attempted speech and upper-body gestures. Personalised machine-learning systems decoded those signals together, instead of treating a spoken message and a physical gesture as separate events.

Two participants used the system to control full-body avatars. That matters because a nod, a shrug or a change in posture can add meaning that a sentence misses.

The researchers also found that simultaneous expression was not simply speech data plus gesture data. Decoders performed better when trained on examples in which both actions were attempted together.

For someone who cannot speak or move normally, a future interface like this might make digital conversation feel more expressive, and give communication partners more context.

Current eye-tracking communication can be slow and tiring, so richer expression could be meaningful.

But this was a wired proof of concept involving only three people. It required brain surgery, and it did not restore biological speech or movement.

The next challenges include larger studies, broader vocabularies, wireless hardware and long-term testing. For now, the achievement is precise but early: one interface decoded attempted words and gestures together.

## Roman — 2:00–3:00

NASA has switched on the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's main camera and successfully communicated with it during commissioning.

The Wide Field Instrument has three hundred megapixels. Between September eleventh and thirteenth, engineers recorded deliberately out-of-focus starlight as an initial baseline.

Its field of view is at least one hundred times larger than Hubble's infrared camera.

That blur was expected, not a failure. NASA also said communication with the coronagraph, plus thermal and mechanical checks, proceeded as planned.

Roman is designed to survey large areas of sky efficiently, helping scientists study cosmic expansion, the distribution of matter and worlds around other stars.

But this was an instrument checkout, not a discovery. Commissioning continues, and first science images are expected in 2027. Roman has not yet mapped dark energy or directly imaged an exoplanet.

## CoRe-VLN — 3:00–4:00

A robot can reach the wrong place and still believe its journey is finished. A team is teaching navigation software to check again.

KAIST and ETRI's CoRe-VLN system rescans its surroundings when it thinks it has arrived. If the destination does not match, it replans the route.

It addresses a failure mode where a robot stops at a visually similar but incorrect place.

KAIST reports a ninety-point-seven percent average across two benchmark tasks. The system ranked first among one hundred and twelve teams, with a tie decided by earlier submission.

Destination verification could eventually make delivery, hospital and factory robots less brittle when layouts, viewpoints or instructions create uncertainty.

But this is a competition benchmark, not a production deployment or proof of universal safety. Real buildings bring people, moving obstacles and rare situations that benchmarks cannot fully represent.

## Atomically thin transistors — 4:00–5:00

Ultrathin electronics require different contacts for different transistor types. A KAIST-led team reports one material that can work with both.

Contacts are a major challenge because ordinary metal deposition can damage extremely thin semiconductor layers or create resistance that wastes energy.

The researchers used a tin-diselenide van der Waals injector with p-type tungsten diselenide and n-type molybdenum disulfide devices.

They also demonstrated a CMOS inverter. KAIST says the p-type device reached a maximum drive current more than one thousand times its nickel comparison.

A shared contact approach could eventually simplify circuits built from atomically thin materials, helping engineers explore smaller, lower-power electronics.

These are laboratory devices, not a new commercial processor. Large-area growth, manufacturing yield, integration and durability still need work, and the reported performance does not apply to every chip.

## Retinal AF signal — 5:00–6:00

Could an eye scan reveal a clue about an irregular heartbeat? UCL and Moorfields researchers analysed retinal images and health records from more than ninety thousand people.

They found thinner retinal nerve layers among people with atrial fibrillation, a rhythm disorder that can be intermittent and difficult to catch during a brief examination.

Because the condition can come and go, a short heart recording may miss it, making additional clues useful.

Among participants diagnosed later, the retinal signal appeared an average of four years before their hospital diagnosis. That is a group average, not a personal countdown.

In future, retinal imaging might help identify people who should receive cardiac assessment when atrial fibrillation has gone unnoticed.

But an eye scan does not diagnose atrial fibrillation or replace an ECG. This retrospective association does not prove cause and effect, and researchers have not established a clinical threshold.
