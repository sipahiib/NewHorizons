Most polar ice loss wasn’t surface melt—it was glaciers speeding into the ocean.

Satellite records show the ice sheets losing mass as frozen rivers carry ice towards the sea.

And this is only our lead story. Four more important developments are coming, from surgical AI to asteroid chemistry. Let’s begin with the biggest.

A new international assessment has reconstructed how the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets changed between 1979 and 2023. The Ice Sheet Mass Balance Inter-comparison Exercise combined forty-two independent estimates drawn from twenty-seven satellite missions, with support from ESA and NASA.

Together, the two ice sheets lost an estimated eleven thousand three hundred and nine gigatonnes of ice, with an uncertainty of five hundred and sixty-five gigatonnes. The researchers calculate that this added thirty-one point four millimetres to global mean sea level, again with a stated uncertainty: plus or minus one point six millimetres.

The striking result is where most of that loss came from. Eighty-four percent was attributed to an imbalance in ice dynamics, mainly glaciers flowing faster and discharging more ice into the ocean. Changes at the surface, including snowfall and melting, accounted for the remaining sixteen percent. So the headline figure must not be described simply as surface melting.

Greenland and Antarctica did not lose ice at a constant rate. Loss varied over time and between regions. A slowdown from 2020 to 2023 is short-term variability, not evidence that the long-term trend has reversed.

Why does this matter beyond the poles? Higher global mean sea level raises the baseline for coastal flooding and erosion. Local effects also depend on tides, storms and land movement. But a longer record helps refine projections used for coastal planning.

This assessment concerns the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, not sea ice or every glacier on Earth. By combining decades of observations and reporting uncertainty, it provides a clearer view of how the planet’s largest ice sheets are changing.
