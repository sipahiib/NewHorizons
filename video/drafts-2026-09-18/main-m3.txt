In Singapore, researchers at Nanyang Technological University have built an artificial leaf that tackles two problems at once. Powered by sunlight, the photoelectrochemical device produces hydrogen from seawater while breaking down hydrazine, a toxic industrial contaminant.

In simulated and real seawater samples, the device maintained stable photocurrent for more than seventy-two hours. NTU also reports that, in one test, hydrazine concentration fell from zero point five molar to zero point five parts per billion within thirty hours.

The idea is attractive because a future system might combine wastewater treatment with solar-fuel production, instead of treating them as entirely separate processes.

However, this is a laboratory-scale result, not a commercial hydrogen plant or a solution ready for deployment at sea. The tests do not yet establish long-term durability, safe industrial scale-up or competitive costs. Hydrazine itself is hazardous and requires careful handling. The work demonstrates a promising chemical pathway; engineering it into a practical system is the next, much larger challenge.
