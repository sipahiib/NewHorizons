## M1 — BepiColombo enters Mercury orbit (from 0:16; target ~120 s)
> On the twenty-first of November, the joint European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency mission BepiColombo fired four thrusters on Mercury's night side and slowed down enough to be captured by the planet's gravity.
>
> Reaching Mercury is one of orbital mechanics' toughest puzzles. Because the planet orbits so close to the Sun, a spacecraft falling inward accelerates rapidly. To enter orbit instead of whipping past, BepiColombo had to shed huge amounts of orbital energy across an eight-year cruise that began in October twenty-eighteen, using nine planetary flybys—including six past Mercury itself. The archive solar-transit footage here from NASA shows how small Mercury sits beside the Sun, while the close-up black-and-white views come from BepiColombo's own monitoring cameras during those earlier flybys, alongside pre-launch cleanroom tests and an archive view of Europe's operations centre in Darmstadt.
>
> BepiColombo is only the second mission ever to orbit Mercury, and the first carrying two separate science orbiters: Europe's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan's Mio, sheltered inside a sunshield. Together, they are designed to study the planet's magnetic field, its dense iron-rich interior, its cratered surface, and ice hidden in permanently shadowed polar craters.
>
> Capture day also brought a real-world engineering reminder. ESA reported that a software issue cut the braking burn shorter than planned. Mission controllers confirmed the spacecraft is safe, and teams are still assessing the exact initial orbit—planned as an elliptical path five hundred and ninety by eleven thousand six hundred and forty kilometres around the poles. Under ESA's arrival plan, the two orbiters are due to separate on the ninth and tenth of December, with Mio's sunshield released around the sixteenth of December and routine science targeted for April twenty twenty-seven. Whether that timetable shifts will become clearer as ESA completes its orbit assessment.

## M2 — EDITH: AI breast screening trial begins (target ~110 s)
> Seven hundred thousand women, and one question: can AI help spot cancer earlier? On the twenty-third of November, Addenbrooke's Hospital in Cambridge became the first of about thirty NHS sites across the UK to begin recruiting women for EDITH—the world's largest clinical trial of artificial intelligence in breast screening.
>
> Backed by ten million pounds from the National Institute for Health and Care Research, the three-year study is co-led by Professor Sian Taylor-Phillips at the University of Warwick and Professor Fiona Gilbert at the University of Cambridge and Addenbrooke's Hospital, working with NHS England.
>
> Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, with around fifty-five thousand cases diagnosed in the UK each year. Across the country, more than two million women aged fifty to seventy-one attend routine NHS breast screening annually. Today, every mammogram is independently read by two specialist radiologists, even though fewer than one percent of screening scans show signs of cancer. While the Cambridge photographs show Addenbrooke's Hospital, the mammography clinic, London NHS hospital exteriors and scan-review clips here are contextual archive footage rather than scenes from the trial itself.
>
> EDITH is testing whether AI-assisted tools can help flag subtle warning signs and safely allow many routine negative scans to be reviewed by one specialist alongside the AI—potentially easing pressure on radiologists so clinicians can spend more time with patients who need care.
>
> Crucially, this is a trial launch, not a finished result. There are no outcome data yet, and it has not yet been proven whether AI improves detection in routine NHS screening. For women invited to take part, the screening appointment itself does not change, and human NHS clinicians remain responsible for every clinical decision.
