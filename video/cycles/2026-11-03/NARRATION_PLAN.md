## Swift

Some footage shows other telescopes for context. NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory launched in November 2004 with a two-year primary mission. It went on to spend more than two decades watching a restless universe.

Swift was designed to catch gamma-ray bursts: brief flashes associated with some of the most energetic events in the cosmos. Because these flashes fade, speed matters. Swift could detect a burst, turn its instruments towards it, and alert other observatories so they could study the afterglow.

Its three telescopes worked across gamma-ray, X-ray, ultraviolet and visible light. That combination helped astronomers connect a fleeting high-energy signal to a longer story about what happened and where.

Swift's mission ended in October, after its orbit fell too low for reliable science. A commercial spacecraft had attempted to reach Swift and raise its orbit, but it did not capture or boost the observatory.

In its November second update, NASA said re-entry was expected on November fourth, with an eleven-hour uncertainty around its estimate. Most of the spacecraft is expected to burn up, though some components could survive. The time and location can change, so check NASA's latest updates rather than treating an early prediction as a precise landing point.

For viewers, Swift's legacy is a reminder that discovery often depends on coordination: one instrument spots an event, and others follow before it fades.

## Hospital AI

Now to a different kind of handover. When someone leaves hospital, a discharge summary helps communicate what happened, which medicines changed, and what follow-up may be needed. Missing information can make that transition harder.

In a university announcement on November second, Stony Brook researchers highlighted a study published in October. They compared AI-generated drafts with human-written summaries for sixty internal-medicine hospital stays lasting seven to twenty-one days.

Hospital doctors and primary-care physicians reviewed paired summaries. The university reports that reviewers preferred the AI version for ninety-five percent of the cases, and scored the drafts more highly for qualities including completeness and readability.

These hospital scenes are contextual; they do not show the research patients or the study itself. The result is promising, but it comes from one hospital and a specific group of longer, complex admissions. It does not prove that patients had better outcomes or that doctors saved time in routine practice.

Both the AI and human summaries contained errors. The researchers found no statistically significant difference in the estimated seriousness or likelihood of harm from those errors. Their conclusion is that a clinician must still check and verify an AI-generated draft before it enters a patient record.

For patients, the practical question is whether future tools can help care teams communicate more clearly while keeping a responsible human in the loop. That still needs real-world testing.
