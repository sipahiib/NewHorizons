## Main opening and lead — flexible tidal turbine blades

Could a rubber blade make tidal power tougher? Researchers have tested a turbine blade that bends with the current. And later, Hubble shows how gravity can turn galaxies into cosmic lenses.

Tidal currents are predictable, but the water flowing through a turbine is rarely steady. Sudden changes in force can repeatedly stress the blades. To manage those loads, large turbines often use mechanisms that adjust blade angle.

Researchers at the University of Edinburgh and their collaborators tested another approach: a rigid supporting section with a flexible rubber trailing edge. As the current increases, the blade changes shape and sheds some of the force acting on it.

The team tested a 1.26-metre model in Edinburgh's FloWave facility. Using the measurements and a simulated tidal cycle, they compared the design with an actively controlled rigid-blade turbine. They report similar energy yield and reductions of up to roughly a third in rapid load fluctuations that can contribute to fatigue.

These are model-scale tests and a simulated comparison, not a long-term trial of a full-sized machine at sea. The researchers suggest that a simpler mechanism could eventually reduce maintenance, but actual savings and durability still need to be demonstrated. If the design scales successfully, it could help make predictable tidal electricity easier to harness.

## Second story — Hubble and Roman

A galaxy can act like a cosmic magnifying glass. NASA has released a gallery of Hubble images showing gravitational lenses: places where the mass of a foreground galaxy bends the light arriving from a much more distant one.

That bending can stretch a distant galaxy into arcs or make it appear in more than one place. By studying the distortion, astronomers can investigate both the background galaxies and the matter in the foreground that bends their light.

Some of these Hubble targets could be followed up by NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launched in August. Roman's wide field is designed to survey large regions of sky, while Hubble can examine individual objects in detail. Together, those different views may help astronomers study how galaxies evolve and how dark matter is distributed.

The gallery shows Hubble observations, not new Roman pictures. And a gravitational lens reveals the effects of mass; it is not a direct photograph of dark matter itself.

## R2 — Flow Country

This wet Scottish landscape is a giant store of carbon. From space, the Flow Country looks like a patchwork of pools and blanket bog. NASA's recently published view was captured by Landsat in 2024.

Here, waterlogged ground slows the decay of plants. Over thousands of years, their remains build up as peat, storing carbon in the soil. The Flow Country in northern Scotland is an unusually extensive example of this habitat, recognised as a World Heritage Site in 2024.

Keeping peatlands healthy matters because damaged or drained peat can release stored carbon. Protecting and restoring these landscapes helps safeguard a store built up over generations. A bog may look quiet, but its history reaches deep beneath your feet.
