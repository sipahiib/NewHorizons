## economical de novo binder design

**_De novo_ protein design campaigns remain bottlenecked by the inference compute time required to**
**generate and rank candidates, limiting wider functional exploration. Here, we introduce and evaluate an**
**epitope excision approach that truncates targets to local interface windows to accelerate both backbone**
**diffusion and structure prediction. Across 15 targets and 3,666 designs, excised windows closely repro**
**duced full-target binding interfaces (median** C𝛼 **RMSD of** 0.37 **Å) and made it practical to design binders**
**against the 719-residue extracellular domain of fibroblast activation protein alpha (FAP) by cutting** **GPU hours by 88%. Pairing this workflow with an agentic optimization loop allowed the system to**
**autonomously identify catalytic-site targeting strategies. Under a fixed compute budget, a human-in-theloop (HITL) workflow raised the computational hit rate from 5.06% to 9.33% while further cutting GPU**
**hours by 42%. We also found that omitting target multiple sequence alignments during refolding roughly**
**doubled the apparent yield. Wet-lab validation with yeast display identified nanomolar binders from the**
**generated designs. Together, target excision and agent-guided search offer a practical way to scale _de novo_**
**binder design to large, therapeutically relevant proteins.**

## Introduction

Structure-based _de novo_ binder design routinely generates thousands of candidate backbones and
sequences per target, combining backbone generators such as RFdiffusion \[1\] with sequence design tools
like ProteinMPNN \[2\] in end-to-end pipelines such as BindCraft \[3\]. Because only a small fraction of
generated candidates actually bind _in vitro,_ campaigns depend on an _in silico_ filtering step in which each
candidate is refolded with its target (for example with AlphaFold3 \[4\], building on AlphaFold2 \[5\]) and
ranked by an interface-confidence metric. One such metric, ipSAE \[6\], is derived from AlphaFold predicted
aligned error and has been shown to outperform earlier scores including pDockQ \[7\] and pDockQ2 \[8\].
How well this computational screen separates true binders from non-binders largely determines the hit
rate of downstream wet-lab testing.

This refolding step is also the primary compute bottleneck. AlphaFold3 has been demonstrated to be a
reliable refolding method, and its runtime is governed by discrete token buckets, with complex lengths
rounded up to the next multiple of 256 tokens. Many biologically important targets, including cell-surface
receptors, multi-domain ectodomains, viral glycoproteins, and enzymes are much larger than the designed
binder and the local patch it contacts. Consequently, most of the tokens processed during refolding belong
to target regions far from the binding site. For a 719-residue enzyme ectodomain of a disease target,
fibroblast activation protein 𝛼 (FAP), full-structure diffusion is nearly intractable and repeated full-complex
refolding is prohibitively slow, even though the targetable epitope is a relatively small rim around the
catalytic opening \[9\].

To get around the compute bottleneck, previous studies have targeted structurally independent folding
units. However, many drug targets containing functional pockets are not confined to a single, small
domain. Recent work has demonstrated that the global fold of a target is not strictly necessary for
generative design, as discontinuous structural fragments can successfully guide backbone generation \[10\].
However, treating target truncation as a penalty-free heuristic without rigorously auditing its downstream

excision: cropping the target down to the residues immediately surrounding the designed interface (the
contact shell plus a tunable surrounding halo) and folding this local pocket in place of the intact protein.
For excision to be useful in practice, it must preserve the local geometry of the designed interface, maintain
the relative ranking of true binders against non-binders, and cross a token-bucket boundary to deliver
actual wall-clock speedups. Instead of treating aggressive cropping as a free speedup, the first half of this
study tests all three requirements systematically across a 15-target benchmark.

Beyond reducing fold times, excision turns otherwise intractable targets into manageable sub-problems.
Running a productive campaign still requires iterative choices about where to target, how much of the
surrounding structure to retain, and how to allocate compute across candidate backbones and sequences.
Large language model (LLM) agents are well suited to this type of iterative, data-driven optimization.
Equipped with external tools, state memory, and quantitative feedback, modern LLMs can plan multistep workflows, launch jobs, inspect intermediate results, and adjust their search policy from round to
round, closely mirroring how a researcher runs a design campaign. Similar multi-agent frameworks built
on Gemini, such as Google’s AI co-scientist, have recently been used to generate, critique, and refine
experimentally validated biomedical hypotheses \[11\]. _De novo_ binder design provides a clean testbed for
evaluating how much autonomy to give such an agent, because campaign performance can be measured
directly as the computational hit rate under a fixed structural threshold and a fixed GPU budget.

In the second half of this work, we hold the excision pipeline, scoring criteria, and total GPU budget
constant, and vary the level of agent autonomy on FAP across three regimes: a human investigator working
alone, a human–agent hybrid (Human-in-the-Loop, HITL), and a fully autonomous agent (Human-out-ofthe-Loop, HOOTL). The two parts of the study address complementary questions: the 15-target benchmark
defines the physical and statistical operating limits of epitope excision, and the FAP campaign tests how
best to direct that pipeline in practice. We find that the human–agent hybrid produces the most highconfidence designs per GPU-hour. Along the way, we also observe that omitting target multiple sequence
alignments (MSAs) substantially shifts AF3 interface-confidence scores on several targets, indicating that
single-sequence refolding can be an effective option depending on alignment depth. Finally, we test these
computational predictions experimentally through yeast display and identified nanomolar binders from
the generated designs.

## Results

The output of a _de novo_ binder campaign depends heavily on computational triage. Because true binders
are rare among generated candidates, every design must be refolded with its target and ranked by an
interface-confidence metric before selecting sequences for synthesis. A natural way to try to improve
this ranking is to spend more compute per candidate by folding each complex with multiple independent
structure predictors and combining their scores. To test the upper bound of this approach, we built
a multi-engine consensus model and evaluated it using leave-one-target-out cross-validation across the
15-target benchmark used throughout this study (3,760 designs total). In this setup, three distinct structureprediction models (AlphaFold3, Boltz-2, and ESMFold2) supply interface-confidence metrics alongside
biophysical features (PRODIGY interface Δ𝐺, FreeSASA buried surface area, and EvoEF2 energy terms)
to a stacked ensemble of linear, multilayer perceptron (MLP), and gradient-boosted models ( **Supporting**
**Figure 1a, Supporting Table** 1). The resulting gain in discrimination is modest: median average precision
(AP) increases from 0.54 using raw AF3 ipSAE alone to 0.60 for the full three-engine ensemble, and the
two steps that require running additional folding models (Boltz-2 and ESMFold2) together contribute only

+0.02 AP while roughly doubling GPU time per design ( **Supporting Figure 1b).** Adding more folding
engines per candidate quickly hits diminishing returns.

If prediction accuracy per fold is difficult to improve by brute force, the more practical way to increase
success rate is to increase screening throughput to enhance selection by reducing the cost of each fold
without degrading binder ranking. Epitope excision **(Figure 1)** approaches this by folding each candidate
binder against a local crop of the target around the intended interface rather than the entire target protein.
In the sections below, we test whether cropped targets preserve interface geometry and binder ranking
across the 15-target benchmark \[13\], define the minimum crop size needed for reliable folding, and then
apply the resulting pipeline to FAP.

For these compute savings to be useful, the cropped target must fold into the same binding interface as
the full-length protein. We evaluated geometric fidelity by measuring the target interface-core C𝛼 RMSD
between the refolded complex and the original design model, using a 3 Å threshold as a validity gate.
Across the full excised benchmark (11 Å halo, 15 targets, 3,666 designs), the pooled median interface RMSD
is 0.37 Å (mean 0.53 Å), with 88.2% of designs falling below 1 Å and 93.7% below 2 Å; per-target medians
range from 0.20 Å (Mdm2 and LTK) to 0.71 Å (EGFR), well below the 3 Å threshold. Because the source
benchmark cached only scalar interface scores for full-target folds rather than full atomic coordinates,
running a direct design-by-design RMSD comparison **(Fig. 2a–c)** required refolding the 394 experimentally
confirmed binders against their intact targets (393 had a measurable interface core). On proven binders,
excised crops match the intact targets closely: median interface RMSD is 0.29 Å for excised targets versus
0.29 Å for full targets, with 98.5% and 97.2% of binders passing the 3 Å gate, respectively. Refolding the
same excised pockets in single-sequence mode (no MSA) yields nearly identical geometry, with a median
RMSD of 0.23 Å, 99.5% of binders under 3 Å, Spearman 𝜌 = 0.83 against full-target RMSD, and only 4
of 393 gate calls differing from the +MSA excised condition ( **Fig. 2a**–c). Per-binder RMSD agreement
between excised (+MSA) and full-target folds is high (Spearman 𝜌 = 0.91, 𝑛 = 393; within the valid <
3 Å subset, 𝑛 = 381, Pearson 𝑅2 = 0.74), with 98.2% agreement on the 3 Å gate ( **Fig. 2a).** The few high-
RMSD outliers, mostly on EGFR, fail in both full-target and excised refolds.

We next tested whether target excision affects the ability of ipSAEmax to rank true binders above nonbinders, treating each target as the unit of replication across all designs (𝑛pos ≥ 5, giving 11 informative
targets). Comparing full-target+MSA folds against excised halo-11+MSA folds, mean AP goes from 0.466
(full) to 0.401 (excised), for a mean ΔAP of −0.065 (paired-bootstrap 95% CI \[−0.130, +0.019\]), while
mean ROC-AUC goes from 0.777 to 0.729, for a mean ΔROC-AUC of −0.048 (95% CI \[−0.083, −0.017\]).
This drop is not uniform across the benchmark; it is driven primarily by a few targets with shallow MSAs or
sensitive local geometry, specifically pMHC\_NY1 (AP 0.50 to 0.02), Pdl1 (0.48 to 0.25), and IL7Ra (0.48 to
0.30), whereas most other targets remain within their bootstrap confidence intervals and IL2Ra improves
slightly ( **Fig. 2d,e).**

for decoys when the pocket fold is simple. Conversely, removing the MSA cuts ranking performance in
half on SARS\_CoV2\_RBD (0.49 to 0.24), a shallow-MSA target that relies on coevolutionary constraints
to fold its interface properly. Whether to include a target MSA during excised refolding should therefore
be chosen on a per-target basis. (Because ranking metrics require both positive and negative examples,
**Fig. 2d,e** includes all designs rather than only confirmed binders.) These trends hold when complexes

Using FAP as a test case, we examined varying degrees of decision-making autonomy by an LLM
agent when steering the excision pipeline (Figure 5c,d). We kept the hit definition (ipSAEmax \> 0.45,
interface-RMSD < 3.0 Å) and the total compute budget (capped at the 132.87 GPU-hours spent by the
human baseline from Arm A) identical across runs, varying only how campaign decisions were made
across three primary conditions: Arm A (fixed excision pipeline as in **Figure 4a;** scientist alone), Arm
B (a human–agent hybrid; Human-in-the-Loop, HITL, in which an LLM agent, Gemini 3.1 Pro, uses the
excision pipeline in **Figure 5c** but chooses the specific epitope sets, crop sizes, ProteinMPNN parameters,
compute allocation, and iteration decisions), and Arm C (a fully autonomous agent that selects its own
targeting strategy and surface patches from scratch without human pre-filtering; Human-out-of-the-Loop,
HOOTL). At each round in Arms B and C, Gemini 3.1 Pro receives a structured summary of per-cluster hit
counts, remaining GPU budget, and current generation settings and selects a single typed action (propose
new epitope or hotspot clusters, iteration/backtrack, epitope/backbone/sequence expansion). We also ran
a fourth condition, the B-variant, which uses the same hybrid autonomy setup as Arm B but modifies two
scoring settings (turning the target MSA off and ranking on ipSAEmin) to separate the effect of the scoring
setup from the effect of agent autonomy.

containing 1,250 designs per campaign arm (Arms A, B, C, and B-variant; **Figure 6b),** augmented with
random mutagenesis (2–3 nucleotide substitutions per sequence) to explore local sequence space. The
library was screened with fluorophore-conjugated target protein across three sequential rounds of fluo
rescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) at decreasing target concentrations (300 nM, 150 nM, and 75 nM).
Deep sequencing via MinION identified 571 enriched unique sequences, including 60 exact matches to
computationally generated designs ( **Figure 6c).** The enriched exact designs comprised 13% from Arm
A (𝑛 = 8), 10% from Arm B (𝑛 = 6), 40% from Arm C (𝑛 = 24), and 37% from Arm B-variant (𝑛 = 22;
**Figure 6d).** Within each arm, the fraction of initial library sequences recovered after sorting was 0.38
(Arm A), 0.83 (Arm B), 0.50 (Arm C), and 0.77 (Arm B-variant; **Figure 6e).** Clonal binding assays on four
representative enriched leads demonstrated nanomolar binding affinities ranging from 3.0 nM to 9.1 nM
𝐾𝐷 ( **Figure 6f).**

## Discussion

This study examines two practical ways to improve the efficiency of _de novo_ binder campaigns against
large protein targets. First, cropping targets to local epitope windows substantially reduces AlphaFold3
refolding costs while preserving both interface geometry (median RMSD of 0.37 Å) and binder ranking
(mean ΔROC-AUC = −0.048). Where ranking accuracy does dip (1 out of 15 targets in our benchmark
dataset), the loss is likely to be concentrated in a small subset of targets with shallow MSAs or fragile
local pocket geometry rather than affecting all targets equally \[10\]. Because AF3 runtime scales in discrete
token buckets of 256, the compute savings from excision depend on target size: small proteins that
already fit in the 256-token bucket see no speedup, whereas large multi-domain targets drop into much
cheaper buckets. The efficiency gains during refolding should apply similar scaling advantages to upstream
backbone generation steps, such as RFdiffusion, that scale quadratically with sequence length. Our halosweep experiments show that the contextual information about the binding interface does affect structural
and ranking accuracy while preserving a structural floor of 60–85 retained target residues, making it a

Second, pairing an excision-based pipeline with an LLM agent improves computational yield under a
fixed GPU budget. Across our controlled comparison on targeting FAP, the human–agent hybrid (Arm B)
achieved the highest hit rate at the lowest compute cost. This result points to a practical division of labor:
having a human researcher specify the general framework for the campaign strategy, such as ranking
metrics and structural properties or biological target regions, while letting the LLM agent handle iterative
parameter selection, crop sizing, and budget allocation across different stages improves throughput over
manual management. Notably, when left to choose its own strategy from a whole-target residue map, the
fully autonomous agent (Arm C) independently converged on the same catalytic-mouth occlusion strategy
and surface-filtering criteria used by the human investigator. Even so, under a capped compute budget,
giving the agent a human strategic prior proved more efficient than having it spend GPU hours discovering
where to bind, whereas full autonomy was useful for spreading designs across a wider set of 13 distinct
epitopes.

To evaluate these computational predictions at scale, wet-lab validation of the _de novo_ FAP binders was
conducted using yeast-surface display. Among the enriched exact sequences, the 3- to 4-fold higher
representation of Arm C relative to Arms A and B suggests that the _in silico_ prediction metrics used by
autonomous agents effectively accounted for expression, whereas relying solely on ipSAE values may
have resulted in reduced expressibility. The improved representation in the Arm B-variant indicates that
enhanced ipSAE in this arm may rescue expression. Furthermore, higher expression and enrichment in the
Arm B-variant demonstrate that excluding the target MSA does not impair binder prediction, consistent
with our _in silico_ hit rates observed in another recent study \[12\]. Finally, the top four clones isolated after
three rounds of sorting exhibited single-digit nanomolar apparent 𝐾𝐷 values, indicating physiologically
relevant and specific interactions with the disease target FAP. Future work will include the binding
characterization of solubly expressed binders, evaluation of FAP domain specificity, and inhibition assays
of FAP catalytic activity.

Several caveats apply to these findings. Refolding excised crops without a target MSA recovers ranking
accuracy on targets with deep alignments but hurts performance on a shallow-MSA target SARS-CoV-2
RBD, suggesting that single-sequence refolding may not apply to all targets. Second, in the FAP autonomy
experiment, the fully autonomous agent in Arm C chose to stop at ∼ 94% of the shared compute budget;
while its final numbers reflect the agent’s own stopping decision, a policy tuned to exhaust the remaining
6% of the budget might have yielded additional hits. Finally, our autonomy comparison focused on a single
large enzyme target (FAP) and a single backbone class (three-helix bundles) driven by one LLM controller;
testing how these autonomy trade-offs generalize to beta-sheet scaffolds and other target classes remains
future work.

Overall, our results outline a straightforward workflow for large macromolecular targets: crop the target to
a local 85–130-residue window (or ∼ 6 Å halo), score candidates with a fixed interface-confidence metric,
and use an LLM agent to manage the iterative design loop within a human-selected target region. Applying
this combination turned the 719-residue FAP ectodomain into a routine computational design campaign
while substantially lowering GPU usage.

## Methods

### Target excision

For each design, target residues with any atom within the contact radius of the binder were retained
together with all target residues within an additional halo shell (keep-radius = 5 Å contact + halo; halos
0/3/6/9/11 = radii 5/8/11/14/16 Å); the pocket was folded in place of the intact target. The crop is
the pure contiguous span of the primary interface chain (no growing or capping in the benchmark
reconstruction). Halo 11 Å is the shipped operating point; halo ≈ 6 Å is the safe floor identified here (≈
9 Å for shallow-MSA targets).

### Folding, scoring, and metrics

Complexes were folded with AlphaFold3 (on-demand memory, --norun\_data\_pipeline, default token
buckets). Ranking used ipSAEmax. Full-target references used a deep MSA and full-target template;
all excised folds in this reconstruction used a per-excision MSA sliced offline from the cached fulltarget a3m. Interface geometry = target interface-core C𝛼 RMSD between re-fold and design reference
(validity gate 3 Å); for geometry-vs-halo curves the median is taken over all designs so failed
folds remain visible, with validity fractions reported separately. Ranking accuracy = AP and ROC-
AUC of ipSAEmax, with per-target values as the replication unit (𝑛pos ≥ 5 for informative targets);
ranking error bars use 2,000-resample design bootstraps, and full-vs-excised deltas use a per-target
paired bootstrap. AF3 runtime was modeled as bucket = ⌈tokens/256⌉ × 256 (minimum 256), with
per-fold times 6.9/17.5/35.0/59.3 s for the 256/512/768/1024 buckets; batch saving per target =
(∑ full-target time − ∑ excised time)/ ∑ full-target time.

### FAP target and excision

The target is the FAP extracellular domain (PDB 1Z68, chain B, residues 39–757; ∼ 719 residues), catalytic
triad Ser624/Asp702/His734 lining a near-buried substrate-channel mouth. The pipeline excises a compact
continuous crop of 85–130 residues around the chosen epitope; excision is frozen as a mechanism across
all arms, with crop size the only tunable knob (a direct compute lever).

$$
39-757;\\sim719
$$

### Design toolchain

Each candidate epitope is turned into binders by a fixed pipeline: RFdiffusion (three-helix-bundle “HHH”
scaffold family only), ProteinMPNN sequence design, AlphaFold3 refolding, followed by refold-based
interface evaluation. Binder length is a free knob hard-capped at ≤ 71 residues. Default generation policy:
ProteinMPNN temperatures {0.1, 0.3} × 4 batches, target MSA on, binder MSA off; the agentic arms may
adjust these.

$$
\\leq71
$$

### Frozen hit metric and budget

A design is a hit iff ipSAEmax \> 0.45 AND target-interface RMSD < 3.0 Å (interface RMSD on a sidechain-bearing refold of the interface core). No arm may redefine a hit; robustness/𝑛core and functionalocclusion diagnostics are computed _post hoc_ and never enter any decision or hit count. All arms share one
currency, measured GPU-hours, capped at the PI campaign’s total (132.87 GPU-h); the controller brackets
each GPU subprocess with a wall-clock timer and refuses any action whose estimated cost would exceed
the remaining budget. Design _count_ is not comparable across arms (per-design cost varies with crop and
binder length) and is reported for reference only.

### Agentic controller (Arms B and C)

A closed-loop controller wraps the frozen pipeline. Each iteration it builds a structured observation (percluster hit counts, budget remaining, current generation policy, never 𝑛core, robustness, or buildability
marks) and an LLM agent (Gemini 3.1 Pro) returns one typed action: propose\_strategy (required first move),
propose\_clusters, configure (GPU-free), screen / expand / deepen / adjust\_crop / backtrack / stop. In Arm B
the PI fixes the strategy and the agent registers only consistent epitope tactics; in Arm C the agent reads a
strategy-neutral whole-target surface map, declares its own strategy, and registers hotspot sets of any arity
anywhere with no buildability pre-filter (every screened set costs GPU-hours whether or not buildable).
The controller is hardened against transient API failures (exponential-backoff retries, bounded timeouts).

n\_{core},

### Diversity metrics

Epitope breadth = number of distinct hit-producing epitopes (unique hotspot-set clusters with ≥ 1 hit).
Sequence diversity = mean pairwise 3-mer Jaccard distance over hit binder sequences (1.0 = maximally
diverse), on a capped random sample per arm. Backbone breadth = distinct (epitope, backbone) pairs
producing hits.

$$
\\geq1
$$

### Yeast display

Yeast surface display screening was performed as described previously by Boder and Wittrup \[14\]. Briefly,
designed protein sequences were codon-optimized for recombinant expression in microbial hosts. For
display on the _S. cerevisiae_ cell surface as Aga2p–Aga1p fusion proteins, coding sequences were extended
with flanking regions homologous to the display vector to facilitate homologous recombination in _S._
_cerevisiae._ Following confirmation of expression, yeast libraries displaying the constructs were enriched
for binding to biotinylated FAP through three sequential rounds of fluorescence-activated cell sorting
(FACS). To prevent non-specific enrichment against secondary detection reagents, the first sorting round
utilized fluorophore-labeled NeutrAvidin, whereas fluorophore-labeled streptavidin was used in subse
quent rounds. For apparent 𝐾𝐷 determination, individual yeast clones isolated from the third round of
screening were incubated with varying concentrations of biotinylated FAP prior to quantification of the
cell-surface binding signal.
