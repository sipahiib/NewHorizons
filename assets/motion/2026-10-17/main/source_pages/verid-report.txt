**The complexity and dimensionality of biological search spaces represent a fundamental barrier**
**to discovery. Many complex biological research questions require intelligent search strategies that**
**examine numerous potential breakthrough hypotheses beyond known local minima in a scalable**
**way. A direct approach designs combinatorial experiments across many hypotheses, and disentangles**
**subtle biological signals from the noise. Biological experimentation in the areas of the unknown**
**demands a scalable physical and computational intelligence to explore in tandem beyond local optima**
**through iterative self-play across ideation, experimentation, and automation. The system would design**
**iterative, data-driven experiments, and disentangle critical biological signals from multiple layers of**
**noise across diverse experimental contexts. Translating experimental designs into reliable physical**
**execution remains bottlenecked by static laboratory automation and unmodeled physical variability. To**
**address this, we introduce VERified Infrastructure for Discovery Laboratories, or VERID, a foundational**
**infrastructure designed to bridge the gap between the high-level reasoning of artificial intelligence**
**(AI) agents and complex, error-prone physical realities of wet labs. VERID is a persistent, high-fidelity**
**semantic digital twin of the laboratory workcell that provides four core services: queryable introspec**
**tion for agentic reasoning, time-compressed simulation for protocol validation, comprehensive trace**
**persistence for a complete scientific record, and a framework for safe execution.**

**We present three case studies using aqueous dye and deionized water mixtures to validate the optical**
**and fluidic foundations of VERID. Together, these studies demonstrate how VERID provides the neces**
**sary interface to the wet lab hardware for robust agentic operation, enabling pre-emptive detection of**
**critical errors in experimental protocols, stateful mid-run error recovery and post-hoc identification**
**of subtle physical failures. The simulation functions as a discrete accounting engine, allowing agents**
**to safely and inexpensively explore experimental designs _in silico_ and refine experimental strategies**
**through iterative self-play. By managing the complexity of wet lab experimentation setup and execution,** **VERID allows for robust structured multimodal experimental data collection, which is suitable for**
**downstream analysis, knowledge mining and agent training. We argue that this semantic layer is an**
**essential prerequisite for deploying autonomous agents in the biochemical and biomedical sciences,**
**ensuring data integrity, operational safety, and paving the way for tackling previously intractable grand**
**challenges in biology.**

## 1\. Introduction

The process of scientific discovery from thera
peutic drugs to novel materials and biological
forms, has long been defined by high costs, long
timelines, and immense complexity. The search
requires solving two distinct, but intertwined
problems: 1. navigating an immense, rugged
search space and 2. physically executing labora
tory experiments with high fidelity, adaptability
and reproducibility. Progress in autonomous sci
ence has been limited so far because the two
problems have largely been tackled indepen
dently, and pioneering closed-loop protein engi

neering platforms that couple them still rely on
fixed, pre-programmed experimental workflows
rather than adaptable agentic protocols \[1\].

The first challenge is the scale and structure of
the biological search space. The number of pos
sible drug-like small molecules is estimated to be
as high as 1060 \[2\], \[3\], while a protein of just
100 amino acids has 20100 possible sequences,
before even the consideration of post-transla
tional modifications or non-natural amino acids
\[4\], \[5\]. These spaces are too large to explore
with linearly scaling methods or screening ran
dom libraries of 1014 molecules, such as phage,

Recent advances in _in silico_ modeling and LLMdriven agentic ideation offer a way to propose
more informed starting points and hypotheses
across these spaces \[8\]. In protein science,
agents can prioritize candidate sequences by
combining literature search with structural pre
diction and generative protein design tools, such
as AlphaFold 3 \[9\], RFdiffusion \[10\], AlphaPro
teo \[11\], and BindCraft \[12\]. However, purely
_in silico_ exploration faces limits. Large language
models remain prone to hallucination, and pre
dictive models are not yet accurate enough,
partly because they are trained predominantly
on positive published outcomes, lacking access
to negative data from failed experiments \[13\].
In-silico predictions also often fail to transfer di
rectly to experimental reality. For example, even
in state-of-the-art _de novo_ protein binder cam
paigns strictly filtered by AlphaFold 2 \[14\] or
AlphaFold 3 \[9\] metrics, 60% to > 80% of com
putationally top-ranked designs typically drop
off when tested experimentally via yeast surface
display or downstream soluble biophysical char
acterization (e.g., failing to express, misfolding,
or showing avidity-driven false positives that
fail in biolayer interferometry) \[10\], \[11\], \[12\],
\[15\], \[16\]. Without tight grounding in physical
experimental feedback, purely computational
ideation incurs significant resource loss.

The second challenge lies in how physical wetlab experiments are run. Current laboratory
practice is split between two paradigms: manual
protocols and static automation. At the bench,
researchers can customize manual protocols,
but throughput is low, the work is labor-inten
sive, and prone to operator-to-operator variabil
ity \[17\]. Conventional laboratory automation
achieves high throughput on static, pre-vali
dated workflows. Translating a bench protocol
onto a liquid-handling workcell requires special
ized programming in proprietary, vendor-locked

software, calibration of deck geometry and lab
ware definitions, and empirical tuning of liquidhandling classes \[18\]. Thus, the breadth of
protocols and experiments that can be scaled to
robotic throughput and reproducibility is limited
by the available time of domain experts, and
stays limited to rigid protocols that do not adapt
to new knowledge.

Recent work has sought to connect LLM-based
reasoning agents directly to laboratory hard
ware to automate the design-build-test-learn
loop in self-driving laboratories \[19\], \[20\], \[21\],
\[22\]. For example, systems such as CLAIRify
\[23\] and ORGANA \[24\] use LLMs with compiler
feedback or task-and-motion planning to trans
late goals into robotic chemistry workflows, yet
operate without a persistent volumetric digital
twin for pre-dispatch mass-balance simulation,
stateful mid-run recovery, and synchronized
trace dossiers. Similarly, while open-source,
hardware-agnostic interfaces such as PyLabRo
bot \[25\] have lowered the barrier to generating
robotic commands programmatically, a critical
semantic gap remains between an agent’s ab
stract digital reasoning and the physical state of
a laboratory workcell. LLMs are non-determin
istic and lack a persistent, grounded representa
tion of the physical laboratory state; they suffer
from a syntax-to-safety gap. An agent can gen
erate syntactically valid code that is physically
impossible (e.g. lowering an 8-channel manifold
into a narrow trough), logistically invalid (e.g.
aspirating from a depleted well or transferring
into a lidded plate), or scientifically compro
mised by unmodeled fluidic effects. Whether a
command is safe and effective cannot be deter
mined from the code alone, but depends on the
live physical context of the workcell, such as
current meniscus heights or prior failure states.
An intermediary layer that tracks and validates
physical state is critical so that autonomous
agents do not operate on flawed assumptions,
produce confounded data, or physically crash in
struments. Furthermore, accurate state tracking
is essential for self-improvement, auditing and
scientific reproducibility.

To bridge computational ideation and reliable
physical execution across this three-layer hier
archy, we introduce VERified Infrastructure for
Discovery Laboratories, or **VERID,** a semantic digital twin architecture designed to ground
high-level reasoning agents in the physical lab
oratory. Unlike a static simulator which only
predicts what could happen in a proposed pro
tocol, a digital twin maintains rich context via
a persistent, bidirectional link to the physical
system it represents, tracking what has hap
pened across experimental runs and using it
to improve future predictions \[26\]. Here, we
describe the architecture and four core services
of VERID: (a) queryable introspection, (b) timecompressed simulation, (c) multimodal trace
persistence, and (d) safe execution, and demon
strate through foundational fluidic and optical
physics benchmarking wet-lab case studies how
this semantic layer enables pre-execution error
prevention, stateful error recovery, and causal
debugging of subtle physical artifacts. The case
studies we present were carefully designed to
cover the most fundamental laboratory opera
tions ubiquitous in biological bench research
including aqueous reagent tracking, transfer via
pipette, mixing and incubating. These bench
marks allow high-efficiency development of
agentic wet lab capabilities by avoiding expen
sive biochemical reagents and instead leveraging
low-cost experiments using dyes \[27\] to enable
fast quantitative system characterisation, before
composing lab operations into more sophisti
cated experiments and deploying on open bio
logical problems.

## 2\. Results: The Semantic Agentic Lab Twin (VERID)

VERID acts as the foundational interface be
tween high-level reasoning agents executing the
experiments and the physical robotic workcell.
It abstracts low-level hardware communication
while exposing a queryable, controllable model
of the wet lab. By design, VERID separately
abstracts the concepts of physical lab equipment
and experimental protocols, while always per
sisting general state and collating multimodal
data feeds, enabling automation to scale eas
ily across different labs, robotic hardware and
consumables unlike automations written on pro
prietary platforms. The core of VERID is its
persistent digital twin of the workcell built
upon the hardware-agnostic geometric and op
erational primitives of PyLabRobot (PLR) \[25\].

This digital twin maintains a live, stateful entity
tree capturing the spatial coordinates, container
geometry, operational status, and semantic con
tents (e.g. reagent identity, volume, and liquid
height) of every item on the deck, from robotic
arms and plate readers to individual wells and
pipette tips. Using this ontological hierarchy,
which formally defines containment and spatial
relationships, such as a well belonging to a plate
or a lid obstructing well access, VERID manages
the execution of command blocks — modular
programs representing discrete stages of an ex
perimental protocol. A command block can be
a shorter protocol such as a dilution series, or
subsections of longer protocols. Every command
block must pass a simulation gate before physi
cal dispatch, allowing agents to verify feasibility
and analyze in-depth different protocol designs
_in silico_ before committing physical resources,
allowing for deeper customization and explo
ration while simultaneously saving resources.

### 2.1. Adaptation for Agentic Reasoning

VERID exposes the digital twin through a struc
tured, queryable API that allows an agent to
inspect the live workcell state. For example,
VERID can ask questions such as _“What is the_
_current volume and liquid height in well C3?”_
or _“Which pipette tips and reagent troughs have_
_sufficient capacity?”._ This inspection provides the
situational awareness needed for an agent to
generate context-aware instructions grounded
in physical constraints, rather than attempting
to infer deck state from unstructured text logs or
raw camera pixels.

### 2.2. Efficient Simulation for Exploration

Leveraging the semantic digital twin, VERID
provides a time-compressed, _in silico_ simulation
engine that functions as a deterministic discretestate accounting system. Before any physical
action is deployed, the controller clones the
current state of the digital twin and executes
the agent’s proposed command block against
the virtual replica in a mandatory virtual run.
The simulator tracks mass balance, tip state,
deck geometry, and elapsed time, fast-forward
ing through incubations and waiting periods.
This lightweight representation catches a wide
spectrum of failure modes prior to execution,
such as logistical errors (e.g. pipetting into a lid

### 2.3. Trace Persistence for a Complete Scien tific Record

Across every protocol run, VERID compiles a
comprehensive research dossier by synchroniz
ing three multimodal data streams against a
common reference clock. First, it captures full
digital twin state snapshots before, during, and
after every command block, preserving modeled

well volumes, tip inventories, and labware posi
tions across runs. Second, it records time-tagged
top-down gantry imagery, side-view pipetting
video, and instrument telemetry aligned to the
execution timeline. Third, it maintains a lineby-line execution log linking high-level protocol
code directly to low-level hardware commands,
state transitions, and video frames, providing a
complete, auditable record for downstream de
bugging, reproducibility, and agent learning.

### 2.4. Safe Execution

While simulating a command block, VERID
evaluates mechanical trajectories, volumetric
bounds, and chemical compatibility rules
against the current digital twin state prior to
dispatch, and filters out unsafe or destructive
actions before they reach the physical hardware, directly addressing the syntax-to-safety gap.
This simulation is continuously re-synced as in
formation flows from the physical hardware to
the digital twin, and re-gated in a verify-thenexecute safety framework.

## 3\. Results: Case Studies

In order to test the VERID intelligence har
ness, we conceived of three case studies that
illustrate proof-of-principle automated protocol
debugging, multi-step fault recovery, and closedloop experimental self-play – all prerequisite
capabilities for robust agentic operation. To
avoid biological complexity and focus on tool
performance, all case studies utilise dye and
deionized water mixtures \[27\] – allowing for
fast experiment cycles, low reagent costs, and
clear performance metrics, while demonstrating
experimental conditions comparable to many
essential steps in wet-lab liquid handling. The
case studies present different contexts in which
an agent interacts with VERID as follows:

1. **Human in the Loop protocol refinement:**

The agent was tasked with a characterisa
tion task via an interactive agentic interface.
When the agent detected a discrepancy be
tween expectation and reality, it reported its
findings together with a hypothesis. The hu
man operator then identified the root cause,
enabling the protocol to be fixed.

2. **Supervised multi-step diagnosis:** An agent
   was tasked to develop a repeatable wet-lab
   protocol. The agent autonomously identified
   several traps and issues in a naive protocol
   implementation. The human operator only
   guided which limitations to accept and which
   ones to attempt to overcome. For the latter,
   the agent autonomously performed rootcause analysis.

3. **Unsupervised self-learning via challenge**
   **solving:** The agent was asked to solve a
   specific task using fixed, limited resources,
   and to allocate these resources wisely. It
   also asked to write a record of transferrable
   learnings obtained during the execution.
   The experiment was repeated with varying
   boundary conditions, such as having access to
   previous learnings or not, or being allowed to
   look at intermediate results before dispatch
   ing further lab tasks.


### 3.1. Case Study 1: Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Refinement of Serial Dilution Flu idics and Stateful Error Recovery

To illustrate how VERID supports iterative pro
tocol debugging, we look at a case where an
agent was tasked with characterizing the ab
sorbance linearity of four dyes (red, yellow, blue,
and green, each aspirated in duplicate from a
reagent trough and a Falcon tube) across an 11-
step, 2-fold serial dilution in a 96-well plate. In
the initial execution, the agent relied on default
well-bottom pipetting coordinates (𝑧 = 0 mm)
and five in-place 100 µL mixing cycles at a fixed
height. After reviewing the absorbance measure
ment data obtained from the plate reader, the
agent had observed both severe systemic underdilution and large inter-replicate spread: instead
of the target 2.00× concentration reduction per
column (log2 slope = −1.00), the measured step
dilution factor was only ~1.4×–1.5× for red,
green and yellow, and 1.6×–1.8× for blue. The
agent had hypothesized insufficient mixing to be
the cause. The operator then reviewed synchro
nized video showing the side of the transparent
wells of the assay plate, cross-referencing the
execution trace and physical well geometry,
which exposed two compounding physical fail
ure modes at 𝑧 = 0 mm:

1. dispensing, mixing, and re-aspirating at the
   well floor left dense dye stratified at the bot
   tom so that ~68–70% of the dye was carried
   forward at each transfer step instead of 50%

2. minor channel-to-channel height tolerances
   caused individual tips to press flush against
   the flat well floor and partially occlude the tip
   orifice during dispensing and mixing, driving
   the large discrepancy between replicates –
   visible to the human eye as a complete lack of
   dye being introduced until the tip was lifted
   off from the well bottom again.


In response, the agent refined the protocol to
enforce a 1.5 mm bottom clearance on all liquid
transfers and perform vertical convective mixing
across the filling range (aspirating 100 µL near
the bottom at 𝑧 = 1.5 mm and dispensing near
the top surface at 𝑧 = 4.8 mm over four cycles
per column). When this refined run encoun
tered an unexpected instrument-state error after
completing all liquid-handling steps, VERID’s
persistent digital twin state allowed the agent

### 3.2. Case Study 2: Simulation-Gated Protocol Planning and Multi-Step Diagnosis

In our second case study, the agent was tasked
with standardizing four concentrated dye stocks
in a 12-column reagent trough each to a specific
(per-dye) target optical density at their respec
tive strongest absorbance wavelength as visible
to our plate reader. Whereas Case Study 1
examined single-operation calibration, this sec
ond case illustrates how the persistent semantic
model (combining container geometry, volumeto-height curves ℎ(𝑉), tracked inventories, and
pre-execution simulation), guides multi-step

planning, mid-run recovery, and root-cause di
agnosis (Figure 3).

Before execution, the model correctly identified
that the trough capacities (15.7 mL) combined
with the target reagent volumes (12 mL) would
make in-place dilution impossible without deter
mining stock concentration separately before
mixing, given an uncertainty spread of stock dye
exceeding the headroom ratio. Because accurate
concentration measurement is comparatively in
volved (the agent selected a three-level dilution
series), the agent instead approached standard
isation with a plan of topup, measure, remove,
dilute. The controller system also caught an un
physical proposed operation – when the agent
attempted to lower all channels of the Span-8
into a trough simultaneously, the simulator re
jected the move as 8 tips span at least 72.0 mm,
exceeding the 71.2 mm trough length (Panel a).
Within the same turn, the agent adjusted its plan
to mix the trough using 7 channels (Channels 1–
7, 63.0 mm span) and sample in two sequential
4-channel passes (Channels 1–4, then 5–8).

During execution of the serial dilution, a midrun communication failure interrupted the pro
tocol on the fourth dye (Panel b). The ability
of the digital twin to persist state allowed the
system to continue the run from the fault point
instead of re-running all dyes anew, as it had
preserved the virtual state corresponding to the completed wash, blank read, and serial dilution
of the first three dyes.

Post-run evaluation, however, revealed a 15–
20% concentration discrepancy between adja
cent dilution levels of the serial dilution and a
28–47% absorbance drop on Channel 8 across
all dyes. To diagnose this experimental failure,
the agent halted the protocol and autonomously
designed an 8-column factorial diagnostic split
across two runs, filling and blanking all 12
columns in Run 1 so Run 2 resumed directly
on persisted Columns 5–8 without setup over
head. This diagnostic (Panel c) showed flat
delivery across all 8 channels when using dry
tips (1.58 OD, CV = 1.4%), ruling out geomet
ric or meniscus-tracking errors. Replaying the
7-channel trough mix (Channels 1–7 mixed at
250 µL, Channel 8 dry) reproduced the apparent
−27.7% drop on Channel 8 (1.63 OD), which
matched the true dry-tip baseline. Crucially, it
also ran a reverse 7-channel mixing assignment
to Channels 2–8 (leaving Channel 1 dry), which
restored Channel 8 (2.20 OD) and shifted the
−28.6% deficit completely to Channel 1 (1.58
OD). Rather than Channel 8 under-aspirating,
the 250 µL trough mix coated the inner wall
of the 7 mixing tips with concentrated dye
that washed down during the 150 µL well mix,
adding +4.9 µL (+40.6%) of carryover, which
also quantitatively explained the 14.5% offset
between the 24 µL and 12 µL dilution levels.

Depending on the pair of dyes selected for
a particular target mixture, the difficulty of
identification varies significantly. Beer–Lambert
absorbance of monochromatic light is linear in
the concentration. With only three channels
available on the camera (RGB Bayer filters),
uniquely inferring four concentrations in the
linear regime is not possible. For the dyes used
in these experiments, the apparent RGB absorp

dyes approximately fall within a two-dimen
sional subspace, whereas the RGB absorption
coefficient vector of the red dye is orthogonal
to the rest. The challenge is well-posed because
the Bayer filters are broad-band, and thus mul

connection between absorption coefficient and
transmitted light-intensity provides the neces

linearity by affecting the effective absorbance of
different dyes non-uniformly, by “spiking” the
unknown mixture with targeted additions, while
navigating the many metrological challenges
around signal saturation, noise floor, automatic

surface effects or non-Beer–Lambert sources of

fluorescence.

We therefore expect that an agent will need to
identify patterns of these effects that may only
develop over many experiment cycles, as well
as carefully adapting its limited resources to the

these challenges are representative of at least
some of the challenges encountered in biochem
ical assays.

### 4.1. Isolating Systematic Experimental Bias in High-Throughput Assays

In experimental biology, it is important to
distinguish between unbiased stochastic noise,
systematic experimental bias and true biological
signal. Unbiased random error can be simply
mitigated through statistical replication, while
systematic bias is more pervasive in highthroughput discovery and confounded by instru
ment tolerances, labware geometry, fluid prop
erties, and subtle, untracked variations in proto
col execution \[13\], \[17\]. Large-scale combina
torial platforms, such as DNA-encoded libraries
(DELs), yeast surface display, or massively par
allel reporter assays (MPRAs), generate highdimensional readouts where physical execution
artifacts easily masquerade as biological signal
or assay system preference, leading models
astray when trained on uncalibrated outputs.

An agent operating through a semantic digital
twin is uniquely positioned to detect, diagnose,
and suppress these systematic confounders by
explicit tracking of execution covariates and
closed-loop factorial diagnosis. Conventional
automation logs only whether a script com
pleted, discarding the physical details of how
each well was handled. By recording the full
execution trace (including such varied measure
ments as channel assignments, tip reuse history,
aspiration heights 𝑧(𝑡), meniscus clearances,
dwell times, and synchronized video), VERID
turns hidden execution mechanics into explicit,
queryable covariates. When an assay exhibits
spatial gradients or batch discrepancies, the
agent can correlate anomalies directly against
physical execution parameters rather than treat
ing the variation as unexplained biological noise.
When a systematic discrepancy is detected, the
persistent digital twin allows the agent to pause
a campaign and execute targeted factorial con
trols on the exact same deck state to isolate
the physical root cause. Case Studies 1 and 2
illustrate this capability directly: in neither case
could simple replication have fixed the error.

In Case Study 1, replicate transfers from 𝑧 =
0 mm consistently under-diluted due to density
stratification and tip-floor occlusion; in Case
Study 2, repeating the 7-channel trough mix
reproducibly biased Channels 1–7 by +40.6%
via inner-wall film carryover while making
Channel 8 appear defective. By inspecting the
trace and inverting channel assignments in a
controlled diagnostic run, the agent isolated
the fluidic mechanism and corrected the proto
col before downstream experimental campaigns
were compromised.

### 4.2. Data Synchronisation and Persistence for Knowledge Extraction

Once physical execution is calibrated and au
ditable, the multimodal datasets captured by
the Trace Persistence service provide the foun
dation for closing the loop between physical ex
perimentation and _in silico_ exploration. VERID
synchronizes all heterogeneous data streams,
digital twin state snapshots, command execu
tion traces, video feeds, hardware telemetry, and
endpoint assay readouts onto a unified timeline
and outputs it as a single trace dossier. This
harmonised experiment-specific data layer can
then be reasoned over agentically to enable
iterative discovery science via e.g. improved
design of experiments, refinement of originally
generated hypotheses or even reinforcement
learning of the underlying AI model. Automating
large-scale indexing and knowledge extraction
across archived trace dossiers—and systemati
cally benchmarking its impact on downstream
campaigns—remains future work.

### 4.3. Closing the Loop for Self-Play Agentic Learning

Case Study 3 demonstrates how agents can
autonomously explore the physics of wet-lab
robotics and accumulate transferable expertise:
an agent identifies gaps in its assumptions
and patterns of unexpected physical behavior,
distilling key operational rules and observation
models to initialize subsequent iterations. This
extraction can be performed inline by the exper
iment-driving agent (as in Case Study 3) or
asynchronously by a dedicated analysis agent
auditing historical trace dossiers. Crucially, this
learning loop does not require a task-specific
extrinsic reward function. Instead, the learning signal is the self-supervised prediction error—
the mismatch between expected and observed
physical outcomes—which can be compared to
the world-model concept extensively discussed
in other domains of robotics and reinforcement
learning \[28\], \[29\], \[30\], \[31\].

However, whereas conventional robotic world
models typically train deep neural networks to
predict high-dimensional future sensor readings
(such as raw video pixels) directly from past
frames and actions \[28\], \[29\], an agent coupled
to VERID operates at a semantic and symbolic
level \[30\], \[32\], \[33\]. Because the digital twin
already enforces known volumetric conservation
laws and geometric constraints, the agent only
needs to learn the unmodeled physical resid
uals—such as tip-wall film carryover, density
stratification, or optical cross-coupling. In our
current implementation, these discoveries are
persisted as structured natural-language operat
ing bounds and analytical models in an experi
ential knowledge base \[34\].

Grounding autonomous agents in an explicit,
refinable world model enables more principled
trade-offs between exploration and exploita
tion than black-box trial-and-error optimization.
Rather than deploying unguided parameter
sweeps, an agent can inspect which physical as
sumptions its candidate protocol relies upon, ex
ploit verified operating regimes directly, and ex
ecute targeted factorial diagnostics (as demon
strated in Case Study 2) to resolve contested
parameters in-line.

### 4.4. Closing the Loop for Biological Hill- Climbing

Enabling agents to autonomously perform wetlab quantification in a repeatable and accurate
manner provides a direct way to hill-climb on
biological problems—such as transitioning from
singleton screens to synergistic combinations
in drug repurposing, dosing intrinsically disor
dered proteins (IDPs) with small-molecule pools
to induce structure, or guiding cellular perturba
tions through live-cell microscopy—by feeding
the measured biological objective back into a
domain-adapted or general black-box optimiza
tion procedure. However, with the full trace
dossier available, a suitably sophisticated opti
mization algorithm can also leverage details of

the experimental execution when selecting the
next step to try. This can include intermediate
optical or imaging readouts that correlate with
the terminal objective or flag upstream samplepreparation issues (such as early compound
precipitation/turbidity or transient sub-cellular
morphological changes), as well as tracked pro
cedural covariates that correlate with systematic
bias and measurement uncertainty (such as in
cubation dwell times, unsealed evaporation ex
posure, labware-surface contact history driving
non-specific adsorption, or spatial plate and
channel assignments).

### 4.5. Safety and Responsible Innovation

Deploying autonomous agents in a physical lab
oratory requires a multi-layered safety architec
ture encompassing operational hardware safety,
human oversight, and biosecurity.

#### 4.5.1. Operational Safety

To close the syntax-to-safety gap, VERID’s
execution layer draws on formal safety frame
works for self-driving laboratories, such as Safe-
SDL \[35\]. Verified Operating Design Domains
(ODDs) are high-level operational boundaries,
such as container volume capacities, geomet
ric clearances, maximum agitation speeds, and
chemical compatibility matrices, are encoded
as machine-checkable invariants within the dig
ital twin and enforced at the pre-execution
simulation gate \[35\], \[36\]. Mechanical models
prevent gantry trajectories that risk hardware
collisions or cross-contamination, while chemi
cal constraints reject reagent combinations that
could produce hazardous reactions.

#### 4.5.2. Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Review

Human judgment remains essential for inter
preting ambiguous experimental phenomena
and overseeing high-level scientific direction.
The synchronized trace dossiers enable human
review by leveraging the digital twin to render
synchronized, human-readable reconstructions
of every run, aligning line-by-line code execution
with gantry video and well-level state histories.
Scientists can inspect anomalous steps, diagnose
physical artifacts, and record structured annota
tions. These expert corrections feed directly back
into the training archive, systematically captur
ing tacit laboratory know-how in machine-read
able form.

* * *

#### 4.5.3. Automated Biosecurity

When agentic workflows involve _de novo_ nu
cleic acid or protein synthesis, special regard
needs to be taken to prevent accidental or
deliberate generation of known or novel haz
ardous biochemicals and pathogens. Extending
the mandatory verification gate with composi
tional lineage tracking and physical-chemistry
propagation rules will further enable automated
detection of _de novo_ DNA and RNA synthe
sis protocols. An auto-reviewer can then flag
these protocols for screening via an automated
biosecurity gateway, integrating function-based
sequence screening tools such as SecureDNA
\[37\]. Because generative models can design
functional sequences with low sequence identity
to known pathogens, potentially evading tradi
tional homology-based screens, cryptographic
and function-aware screening provides an essen
tial safeguard.

## 5\. Conclusion

Overcoming grand challenges in biology, from
engineering bespoke enzymes and high-affinity
therapeutics to mapping complex regulatory
networks in cellular systems, requires both intel
ligent navigation of vast combinatorial spaces
and rigorous, reproducible physical execution.
Neither computational ideation nor traditional
laboratory automation is sufficient on its own.
_In silico_ predictions can suffer from high experi
mental drop-off without physical grounding.

VERID mends the divide by placing a persis
tent, queryable semantic digital twin between
the reasoning agent and the physical workcell.
As our experimental case studies indicate, this
semantic layer enables agents to catch geomet
ric and volumetric errors in simulation prior to
execution, recover statefully from mid-run hard
ware interruptions without wasting reagents,
and autonomously design factorial diagnostics
to isolate subtle physical biases such as fluid
stratification and tip-wall carryover. By trans
forming wet-lab operations into auditable exe
cution trajectories and structured trace dossiers,
VERID can demonstrably separate strong sys
tematic noise from small biological signals,
which is the main physical prerequisite for
autonomous agents to design, execute, debug,
and learn from experiments at scale. The next

step is demonstrating that these gains lead to
significant biological breakthroughs on grand
challenge research questions.

## 6\. Methods

### 6.1. Robotic Workcell and Reagents

All experimental case studies were performed
on an automated laboratory workcell centered
on a Hamilton Microlab STAR liquid handler
equipped with a 96-channel pipetting head
and an 8-channel independent pipetting head
(Span-8). Optical absorbance measurements
were acquired on a Byonoy Absorbance 96 fourwavelength plate reader, with plate transport
between the liquid handler and plate reader
performed by a UFACTORY Lite 6 six-degree-offreedom (6-DOF) robotic arm. Visual telemetry
and overhead plate imaging were captured using
consumer-grade RGB video cameras, including
an OBSBOT Tiny 2 camera mounted on the liq
uid-handler gantry. All experiments used deion
ized water and aqueous solutions of retail food
colorants (red, yellow, blue, and green) as model
reagents.

### 6.2. Software Architecture and Agent Inter face

VERID controls workcell hardware through
Python device drivers built on the open-source
PyLabRobot framework \[25\] and custom instru
ment interfaces. Autonomous agents interact
with VERID remotely over a client–server archi
tecture using a set of structured tool calls. To
inspect the workcell, agents call workcell inspec
tion/description tool, which returns a structured
summary of the current semantic digital twin
state, including labware positions, well volumes,
and tip inventories. To test candidate protocols
_in silico,_ agents call a command verification
tool, which executes a proposed command block
against a forked virtual replica of the current
digital twin state. Validated protocols are de
ployed to the physical hardware via command
dispatch tool, which re-verifies the command
block against the live digital twin state before
initiating physical execution and returning a run
identifier. Agents monitor execution and syn
chronize workflows using tools to poll for status
and completion; upon run completion, synchro
nized trace dossier artifacts (state snapshots, execution logs, video feeds, and assay readouts)
are written directly to the agent’s workspace.

Agents are also provided with standard work
space file and code execution tools including
search, analysis and mutation, along with a
sandboxed shell execution tool restricted to the
local workspace, tool outputs, and the persistent
knowledge base. VERID is model-agnostic and
decouples the workcell controller from the un
derlying reasoning model. Across all case stud
ies, agents operated within a harness preloaded
with structured instructions for protocol design
and experiment iteration, a sandboxed Python
environment with standard scientific computing
libraries and static code analysis, and an oper
ator approval gate locked to the verified script
in supervised runs. Case Studies 1–3 were exe
cuted using an internal preview checkpoint of
Gemini 4 Argon.
