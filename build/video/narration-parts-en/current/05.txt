The Arctic sea-ice melt season is about forty days longer than it was in nineteen seventy-nine. Yet its average length appears to have stopped increasing around twenty ten.

A NASA-led study analysed satellite observations through twenty twenty-three. Earlier extension came mostly from later autumn freeze-up, as darker open ocean absorbed more solar energy.

Since twenty ten, changing cloud patterns have reduced incoming sunlight in parts of the Arctic. Year-to-year weather now has greater influence over ice that is generally younger, thinner and more vulnerable.

This does not mean Arctic warming has stopped. Melt-season length is only one measure; remaining ice is much thinner, and the region continues to warm far faster than the global average.

Researchers warn the pause may be temporary. Continued thinning north of Greenland and the Canadian Arctic could trigger rapid change. The real lesson is that a flat trend can hide a fragile system.
