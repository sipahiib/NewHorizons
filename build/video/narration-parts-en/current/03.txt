In Singapore, Nanyang Technological University researchers built an artificial leaf that tackles two problems at once. Powered by sunlight, the photoelectrochemical device produces hydrogen from seawater while breaking down hydrazine, a toxic industrial contaminant.

In simulated and real seawater, it maintained stable photocurrent for more than seventy-two hours. NTU reports hydrazine falling from zero point five molar to zero point five parts per billion within thirty hours.

A future system might combine wastewater treatment with solar-fuel production.

However, this is a laboratory result, not a commercial hydrogen plant. The tests do not establish long-term durability, safe industrial scale-up or competitive costs. Hydrazine is hazardous and requires careful handling. The chemical pathway is promising; engineering a practical system is the larger challenge.
