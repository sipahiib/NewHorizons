A brain implant just decoded speech and body language at the same time. The result points toward assistive communication that could express more than words alone.

Four more important developments are coming, from a space telescope to robotics, ultrathin electronics and eye scans. Let's begin with the biggest.

At the University of California, San Francisco, researchers worked with three people who had paralysis. Each already had electrodes placed on the brain's surface.

Those electrodes record patterns from the motor cortex. They do not read every private thought. The system was trained for specific attempted phrases and familiar gestures, including a wave and a thumbs-up.

The participants attempted speech and upper-body gestures. Personalised machine-learning systems decoded those signals together, instead of treating a spoken message and a physical gesture as separate events.

Two participants used the system to control full-body avatars. That matters because a nod, a shrug or a change in posture can add meaning that a sentence misses.

The researchers also found that simultaneous expression was not simply speech data plus gesture data. Decoders performed better when trained on examples in which both actions were attempted together.

For someone who cannot speak or move normally, a future interface like this might make digital conversation feel more expressive, and give communication partners more context.

Current eye-tracking communication can be slow and tiring, so richer expression could be meaningful.

But this was a wired proof of concept involving only three people. It required brain surgery, and it did not restore biological speech or movement.

The next challenges include larger studies, broader vocabularies, wireless hardware and long-term testing. For now, the achievement is precise but early: one interface decoded attempted words and gestures together.
