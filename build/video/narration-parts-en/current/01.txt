Perseverance found a hidden water history written inside volcanic rock.

At the edge of Mars’s Jezero Crater, the rover examined the Margin Unit. Scientists expected layered lake-shore sediment. Instead, they found igneous rock altered repeatedly by water.

And four other breakthroughs happened this week: a cybersecurity AI project, factory automation plans, a dissolving medical battery, and a surprising Arctic trend.

The story begins with olivine-rich rock formed from magma deep underground. At higher elevations, its large crystals show almost no water alteration. Lower down, the olivine is fractured and transformed.

SuperCam examined many bedrock targets. Its laser creates plasma, revealing rock chemistry from several metres away.

That chemistry records at least three water episodes. First, carbon-dioxide-rich groundwater reacted with olivine and left carbonate inside fractures. A later interaction may have involved the lake that once filled Jezero Crater, leaving more silica below the ancient water line.

Finally, heated groundwater circulated through volcanic rock and produced mineral veins containing calcium sulfate and fluorite.

Carbonate and silica matter because, on Earth, they can preserve traces of ancient environments. Reactions between water and olivine can also release hydrogen, an energy source for some microbes.

But this is not evidence that life existed on Mars. The minerals document water interacting with rock, and scientists cannot yet date the three episodes. What the rover has revealed is a more complicated sequence of groundwater, lake water and hydrothermal activity than orbital observations alone suggested.

One place on Mars did not have a single water story. It became a crossroads for several.
