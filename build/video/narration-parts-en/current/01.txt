FAST just mapped 156,411 cosmic hydrogen sources. Built into a natural basin in southwest China, this enormous radio dish has surveyed nearly half the sky. Four more important developments are coming. Let's begin with the biggest.

On September eighth, the FAST survey team released its second public data set. It contains radio detections of neutral hydrogen beyond our own galaxy, gathered between 2020 and 2025.

Neutral hydrogen is not a message from space. It is the simplest form of the universe's most abundant element, and a basic reservoir from which galaxies make new stars. It emits a faint radio signature with a wavelength of about twenty-one centimetres. Unlike visible light, that signal can pass through clouds of interstellar dust.

FAST's huge collecting area is sensitive enough to catch those weak emissions across the nearby universe. The new catalogue gives each detection a position and measured signal, allowing astronomers to map where galaxy-forming gas is concentrated.

The real advance is scale. Researchers can compare gas-rich and gas-poor galaxies, study how matter is arranged across cosmic structures, and identify targets for follow-up observations. The catalogue is publicly available, so teams around the world can work with the measurements without repeating the entire survey.

Researchers can cross-match the radio catalogue with optical surveys, linking hydrogen to visible galaxies.

But this is a data release, not proof of a new cosmological theory. Its sources mostly belong to the relatively nearby, low-redshift universe, and each entry should not be described as a newly discovered galaxy. Even so, one telescope has turned years of faint radio signals into a shared map of the raw material of stars.
