A battery inspired by edible rice paper could make temporary medical electronics safer.

Researchers built a cellulose-supported battery from magnesium, molybdenum trioxide, biodegradable electrolyte and beeswax. In pigs, it powered an identification tag that transmitted a signal outside the body. A second device electrically stimulated the stomach and increased levels of the hunger-related hormone ghrelin.

In simulated gastric fluid, the battery degraded over months. The goal is to avoid batteries that can leak corrosive or toxic materials.

One day, this approach might power ingestible sensors or temporary implants without requiring removal.

But the experiments were performed in pigs, not people. They do not establish clinical benefit, long-term human safety or regulatory approval. The battery is a promising preclinical power source—not a treatment ready for patients.
