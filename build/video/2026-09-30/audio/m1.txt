Earth bent Juice’s path toward Jupiter and saved fuel. Why did a Jupiter probe come back to Earth? And four other breakthroughs happened this week: a new AI model, a Japanese hydropower upgrade, heat-tolerant vaccine research, and a fresh way to test wind turbines. On September twenty-eighth, the European Space Agency's Juice spacecraft swept past Earth above the Indian Ocean. It was not returning home. Earth was one carefully timed stop in a long route to Jupiter. ESA says the flyby bent Juice's path by twenty degrees and increased its speed by three point five kilometres per second. The team had spent weeks tracking it closely, because a tiny error in approach could change the outcome. In the month before the encounter, controllers used just one small correction out of six opportunities they had reserved. The result matters for a spacecraft with a limited tank of propellant. Using a planet's gravity to reshape the route can leave more fuel for manoeuvres when Juice reaches the Jupiter system. During the pass, its monitoring and navigation cameras photographed Earth and the Moon. These are real spacecraft images, though the monitoring cameras were built to check the craft's own hardware, not to take polished science portraits. The flyby also gave instrument teams a chance to calibrate equipment against a world they already know. The team expects images and spectra from the science instruments after their checks are complete. Those science observations are still being evaluated; this pass did not tell us whether Jupiter's moons harbour life. Juice will swing by Earth again in twenty twenty-nine before its planned arrival at Jupiter in twenty thirty-one. There, it is designed to examine the giant planet and its ocean-bearing moons. This week's achievement is navigation: a precise encounter here that makes more ambitious observations possible far away.
